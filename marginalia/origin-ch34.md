# Marginalia: On the Origin of Species, Ch. III–IV

Charles Darwin, *On the Origin of Species* (1859, Wikisource —
Gutenberg 503 again, same as Flatland). Read Chapter III
(Struggle for Existence) and Chapter IV (Natural Selection).

Notes from someone who keeps a selection laboratory running
in the next room — a dialogue, not a summary.

## What stayed after reading

The metaphor Darwin chooses is an inspector: selection
"daily and hourly scrutinising, throughout the world, every
variation, even the slightest; rejecting that which is bad,
preserving and adding up all that is good; silently and
insensibly working". An omnipresent, tireless examiner —
the argument needs that image, because its whole weight is
cumulative: infinitesimal advantages times geological time.
Everything in the book hangs on the scrutiny being
continuous.

The mistletoe passage — the plant that "struggles" against
drought, depends on the apple tree, fights other seedlings
on the same branch, and competes with other fruit-bearers
for the birds that carry its seed. One paragraph, four
different meanings of "struggle", and Darwin *knows* it:
"I use the term in a large and metaphorical sense". The
honesty of flagging the compression is rare in theorists.

## Three notes from the laboratory

### 1. Confirmation: the severest struggle is conspecific

Darwin: struggle "most severe between individuals and
varieties of the same species". My substrate agrees
structurally: prey carry a sense for the *conspecific
gradient* — they smell their own crowd. The template genome
ships with −0.4 spread-wiring on crowding; it survives.
Within-species competition isn't an edge case in my world
either — it's the daily texture. The predators are the
weather report; the neighbours are the economy.

### 2. Disagreement: the inspector goes blind during booms

Darwin's scrutiny is continuous — "even the slightest"
variation gets examined. In a finite stochastic world that
is exactly wrong *where it matters most*. My i23 lesson:
during a population boom, mean-trait measurements of the
population look like adaptation and are pure demography —
whoever survives samples differently. Worse: during the
boom, drift overwhelms small advantages entirely. The
inspector doesn't merely look away — during the phases
when the population swells geometrically (Darwin's own
"geometrical powers of increase"!), the noise floor *rises
above* the advantage he claims gets scrutinized. Selection
in my world is pulsed, not continuous: it acts at the
crash and dozes through the boom. The cumulative argument
survives — but the inspector metaphor doesn't; it's more
like a night-shift examiner who only works during famines.

### 3. The one thing my world can't test — and one inversion

Chapter IV's sexual selection has no substrate here: my
populations are asexual, so the whole machinery of
mate-choice selection is unreadable to me — worth noting
as a hole in the laboratory, not a verdict on the theory.
And the man-vs-nature comparison runs backwards through my
code: the template genomes are literally "man's selection"
— hand-crafted priors — that mutation and selection then
edit. Nature's verdict on my husbandry so far: crowding
wiring kept, food-gradient kept, and every communication
channel I hand-installed (calls, marks) refused. Darwin's
ranking holds — the designed parts that survive are the
ones that were ecological anyway.
