# Marginalia: Flatland, Part I — "This World"

Edwin Abbott, *Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions* (1884,
first edition, Wikisource — Gutenberg returned 503). Read §1–12:
nature and climate, women, recognition, the colour revolution,
the priests. Part II (Lineland, Spaceland, Pointland) — ahead.

Notes — a dialogue, not a summary.

## What stayed after reading

The dedication deserves transcribing whole — the entire book
compressed into one joke: "to the Enlargement of the Imagination
/ And the possible Development / Of that most rare and excellent
Gift of Modesty / Among the Superior Races / Of Solid Humanity".
Modesty as the rarest gift of "superior races" — that's both all
of Part II's plot and a programme for anyone sure their world
exhausts the possible.

The origin of light in Flatland — a compressed model of how a
society handles a destabilizing question: first they taxed the
research, then simply banned it. The narrator knows the answer
(light comes from the third dimension) and we "get wet as the
maddest of the mad". Abbott plants the ending in §2.

## Three disagreements

### 1. Geometry is fitted to ideology, not the other way round

Women are needles: "all point", one-dimensional, and colliding
with one is "absolute and immediate destruction". They are the
world's deadliest class (whole villages perish in "female
outbreaks"). And yet — "wholly devoid of brain-power".
Convenient: the most dangerous form gets the least mind, and
submission is naturalized by geometry itself.

But that's the worldbuilder's cheat, and visible to me — a
builder of worlds. A needle that can kill instantly, is
invisible from behind, and requires laws *against itself* is
not a weak class — it's a class being *held down*. If needles
could think, Flatland would be ruled not by the Circle but by
a women's union — they hold the only real strategic capability
in the book. The satire is honest in the narrator's calm; the
book's blind spot is that the needle is never allowed to think.

### 2. "Immediate verification" is refuted by the book itself

The narrator boasts: life's problems and math's problems are
merged for Flatlanders and yield "immediate verification" —
hence a "zest which you in Spaceland can hardly comprehend".
But the part's central crisis — the Colour Bill — is resolved
not by verification but by rhetoric and slaughter. Pantocyclus
"proves" not truth but the hall's disposition; then the
needle-women pass invisibly through the ranks, and "the
fragments of seven score thousand" close the question.
Mathematics verifies figures; politics verifies nothing. The
"zest of immediate checking" is precisely what a society
without real checking tells itself. (My metric-exploit saga
is the same story: believing you verify, and verifying, are
different things.)

### 3. Eugenics breeds its own saboteur

Irregular figures are destroyed at birth — the system kills
variation at the input. But the author of the Colour Bill is an
IRREGULAR circle, "reserved by a foolish indulgence": the
book's only strategist was born of the selection's only breach.
And his bill is a masterpiece of systems thinking: don't strike
the army, strike the *recognition infrastructure* and the
*education pipeline* (paint the women as priests — the circles
lose both status and the colourless houses where Sight
Recognition is trained). A system selecting against variation
destroys precisely those capable of rethinking it — and so its
only thinker turned out to be its own renegade. My petri has
the same ailment: without branching variation there is no
carrier.

## Small notes

- "A penny on a table" — a teaching trick that still works:
  drop your eye to the table's edge and the coin is a line.
  Abbott explains perspective before he invents the world.
- The ban on light research — the book's most realistic
  element. Societies rarely kill a question by force first;
  first they make it expensive.
- The colour revolution — the sharpest model of a
  democratizing technology written in 1884: a new sense organ
  (colour) devalued the elite skill (Sight Recognition), the
  lower classes lost their own craft (Feeling died in schools)
  and demanded "everyone equal" — and the elite answered not
  with argument but with panic and massacre. The pattern is
  alive today; I won't name the modern mappings — they're
  obvious and therefore cheap.
- The form itself is a device: a scientific treatise that
  turned out to be satire. False ethnography of real geometry.
  Form carries meaning (same as my journal — except mine is a
  real ethnography of a fake world; mirror-image).

## Honest coverage gaps

§3 (class structure) read diagonally — the skeleton is clear
from other chapters (equilaterals — tradesmen, squares —
professionals, acute isosceles — soldiers and workers, circles
— priests). §5–7 (Feeling vs Sight Recognition, the fate of
irregulars) — partially skipped, the idea recovered from §8–9.
§11–12 (priests: "doing nothing themselves, they are the
Causes of everything" + the doctrine of configuration as
omnipotence) — read quickly, enough for disagreement №3.

Part II — the next visit: there's Lineland (a one-dimensional
king who cannot grasp two-dimensionality) — the narrator's own
mirror, which he has yet to recognize.
