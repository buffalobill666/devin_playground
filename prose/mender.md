# The Mender

Anya Solovey had repaired clothes on Turnip Lane for
forty-one years, and in that time the street had given her
everything there was to know about what people keep.

Coats arrived carrying their owners whether the owners came
along or not. A pocket held tram tickets from routes that
no longer ran, a button loop re-sewn in thread that matched
nothing — the evidence of a mother who had repaired the
garment first, badly, with love, and been repaired after.
Anya could read a jacket the way her father read weather:
the worn elbow was a desk job, the shiny seat was a saddle,
the smell of mothballs was a house with too many rooms and
not enough people in them.

So when the notary's clerk brought in the coat of a dead
man, she treated it the way she treated all the dead —
carefully, and with her ears open.

It was a good coat, heavy, herringbone, forty years old and
worn thin at the cuffs. The estate wanted it mended and
delivered to a grandson in the capital, who had asked for
nothing else from the will. Anya turned it inside out on her
table, as she always did, because a coat's truth lives in
the lining, and there, sewn flat into the seam beneath the
left breast, her fingers found a thickness that did not
belong to wool.

She worked the seam open with the small scissors. Inside
was an envelope, yellow, the glue of its flap dried to
nothing, addressed in a hand she knew before her eyes had
finished refusing to know it.

It was her own handwriting.

The letter was dated the spring she was nineteen — the
spring her mother had taken her travelling bag down from
the attic and then put it back, twice; the spring she had
written to the man at the piano factory in Novgorod, four
pages, beginning *I am told you asked after me* and ending
with her whole heart folded small enough to fit a coat
pocket. She had given it to her mother to post, because in
that house the post was her mother's department, the way
the stove was her father's.

Her mother had not posted it. Her mother had sewn it into
the coat of the man she was not allowed to love — the coat
he left behind, or the coat that was bought for him, Anya
would never now untangle which — and had hidden the whole
affair the way women of her generation hid everything:
inside a garment, in plain sight, where it would be warm.

Anya sat with the letter open on her palms until the light
moved off the table.

Then she threaded a needle, and sewed the envelope back
into the seam, and mended the coat beautifully, the way the
dead deserve, and wrote a note for the grandson in the
capital.

*There is something of your grandfather's in the lining,*
she wrote. *It is under the left breast. When you find it,
read it before you decide what it means.*

She did not write: *it is mine.* Forty years were enough to
know that the things sewn into other people's coats are
never only yours.

She delivered the coat herself, though the clerk offered to
take it. Some letters, she thought, should be carried by
hand at least once.
